The fabric

Absorbent core and waterproof barrier bonded into a single cut. One piece of fabric, not three layers — less sewing, less training, fewer ways to get it wrong in a workshop.

Why polyester, and why silver

These pads are hand-washed in cold water, often without soap, and dried in the open air. Cotton — the instinctive "natural" choice — holds moisture and won't dry fast in humid climates; polyester pushes water out and dries quickly. Silver Plus follows the same logic: where washing is imperfect, inhibiting bacteria on the fabric itself matters more, not less. Both calls were Denys's own, made for the conditions Shortee actually works in.

All specifications carry ±10% per textile manufacturing standards; we do not test absorbency in the finished, sewn product.

The Story

Every hand that came before

You are not the first

Somewhere right now, there is a shoebox being packed on a folding table in a church basement. Inside it, a small stack of pads — sewn by a woman who will never meet the girl who opens the box. She has made her peace with that. She is not the first, and neither is Shortee.

This company has been doing this work for more than a decade, through the hands of women who mostly don't know each other, sewing the same fabric into the same kind of dignity, again and again. A few of their names:

Pamela Killion — ten years, one fabric, one company.
Donna Terpstra — twelve unbroken years, sending barrier fabric to women's empowerment programs.
Cynthia S. — four years of Operation Christmas Child shoeboxes, sealed on faith they'll arrive.

The same hand

Makers who have never met arrive at the same choice

Teresa chose a dark brown barrier fabric for her Operation Christmas Child kits — the color would carry staining the girls couldn't wash out, in conditions where washing itself couldn't be guaranteed. She designed for a life she would never see.

waterproof (bleedproof)

— Kathleen Tiffin, choosing the same dark, Silver-treated fabric years later, on another continent, for Ghana

Two words, and a technical spec had already been translated into the language of the people she was sewing for. That's not coincidence. That's what the constraint teaches everyone who actually listens to it.

Fifty yards

The arithmetic is the whole point

Dianne Leach wrote it down first: fifty yards of fabric does one thousand pads. It isn't a specification. It's the shape of what this work actually is — every yard converts, through someone's hands, into a unit of dignity.

50 yds → 1,000 pads
Dianne Leach's own arithmetic
25 yds → 250 pads
Rebecca Blackburn, sewn whole into Operation Christmas Child shoeboxes

One maker, SCL, kept a roll going toward a school in South Sudan after a real manufacturing flaw — delamination, mid-roll — and wrote that it was still exactly what we need. The commitment survived the flaw. That's the standard here: not perfect fabric, fabric that holds.

The hidden chain

Nobody sees the whole chain. It holds anyway.

Maker→ Pad→ Shoebox→ Shipping→ Distribution→ A girl's hands

At every stage, the work is hidden from the next. The maker never learns whether it worked. The girl at the far end can't write back — no internet, no review platform, no way to close the loop.

Their silence is not missing data. Their silence is the confirmation the grace reached them.


Every maker in this community sews into that silence on purpose. They never expected a reply. The work was the prayer.
