What They Made

We didn’t decide who these people are. They told us, for eleven years.

Why this page exists

Every other map we have is us describing ourselves — what we intend to write, what we intend to film, who we believe our customers are. This one is the other direction. It is made entirely of what customers wrote and photographed, unprompted, between 2015 and today. It shows what they have already proven, and where the picture is still missing.

Where you’ll be when you’ve read it

You’ll know these are real people making real things, with their names on them. You’ll know which parts of the range they have already proven and which they haven’t. And you’ll know exactly where one photograph would change something — with the reason it is missing, not just the fact that it is.

How this is counted

Everything here is theirs, not ours

Two bodies of material, kept separate because they behave differently.

What people said. 6,829 written voices across six places they reached us — our own store, Google, Etsy, our wall of customer stories, and the box at checkout where people tell us what they are about to make. Two-thirds of those people wrote a review after the fact; the rest told us their intention before they had sewn a stitch. Only 390 did both.

What people showed. 931 photographs and videos customers posted or sent us — mostly Instagram, some Etsy, some Google, and 39 that simply arrived by email because somebody wanted us to see what they’d made.

What we did with it. We matched both to the 182 things a person can make from our cloth. Where the match was clear, it is linked and you can open the record. Where it wasn’t, it is left blank and counted. A hole on this page is a real hole — we would rather show you one than fill it with a guess.

Two honesties about dates. Most checkout notes carry the date we imported them, not the date they were written, so nothing on this page reads a trend from those. The timeline further down uses only the 1,022 reviews that carry a real date. And the industrial sorbent side of the business is not on this map at all — different customers, different proof, a different conversation.

Written by us, never photographed

Four pages carry our text and not one customer picture. Adult Bibs. Dental Bibs & Procedure Drapes. Diaper Pail Liners. Pet Crate Pads & Liners.

Three of the four are institutional or clinical. Makers photograph what they made for a person they love; nobody photographs a dental drape. The fourth is the one worth a second look — diaper pail liners sit in the middle of the most photographed community we have, and still drew nothing.

What the shape says

The corpus is domestic

Set the light communities beside the dark ones and the pattern is not random. People photograph babies, periods, pets, kitchens and bags. They do not photograph athletic wear, cars, the outdoors, or a gym.

That is not a gap in our collecting. It is a fact about who takes the photograph. The domestic maker is making something for a person they love, and the picture is part of the giving. The performance buyer is making something to work — and a thing that works does not get posted.

Which is why the dark half is not an absence of customers. In Athletic & Performance Wear, four applications have people telling us at checkout exactly what they are making — leggings, sweatbands, athletic tops — and not one photograph exists. They are here. They buy. They don’t post.

For the people who help us tell this

Where the picture is missing

Three asks, and they are different from each other. We are not asking for ideas about where customers might be. We already know where they are. We are asking for help getting the picture.

One · the harvest

33 applications where customers state at checkout what they are making, and no photograph exists. These are not prospects. They are buyers, named, with the cloth already in their hands. The whole list is below.

Two · routes we already have

39 pictures arrived by email, unprompted, because someone wanted us to see. Nobody designed that. And referral is already live in the record — one maker appears four times as the stated reason for a purchase; another customer simply wrote “Starfish Emb sent me.”

Three · two streams, kept apart

What we hold is maker-made, product-in-use — playful, handmade, on a kitchen table. Lifestyle is a different register with a different source: commissioned or influencer, and it will not arrive free. Quoting for one when we meant the other is the expensive mistake.

And the four communities that are genuinely dark

Automotive & Outdoor — 5 of 5 applications with neither a word nor a picture. Outdoor & Specialty — 6 of 7. Medical & Healthcare — 12 of 15. Gym, Fitness & Sports Gear — 8 of 10.

These are not the same problem as the harvest list. Here there is no photograph and no maker statement, which means either we are not reaching those people or they arrive through a door we haven’t built. That is a strategy question, not a content one, and it is the one we’d most like to think about together.

Coming home

A picture is the shape of caring

Kathleen Tiffin’s nine words about girls in Ghana are not marketing material. Neither is klay arsenault’s teenager with ulcerative colitis, or Danielle Koch measuring a guinea pig cage at two feet by four. They wrote those sentences into a checkout box because somebody asked what they were making, and then nobody read them for years.

This page is the reading. It is also the first honest count of what we can and cannot show — and the four dark communities are on it deliberately, because a proof page that hides its holes is not proof.
