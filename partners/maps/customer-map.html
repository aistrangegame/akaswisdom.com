If you read one thing

Two businesses share one family. Makers of every size buy cloth by the yard and climb a seven-rung ladder toward the roll. Manufacturers and distributors buy mats and rolls to a spec and never touch the ladder at all. The nine kinds of customer are where people stand on those two walks — and the closer a thing is to someone's skin, the more likely the person who makes it is the one buying the cloth.

Two questions sort every customer. What do they do with the cloth — ask for it, make with it, carry it on, cut it up, or buy it to a spec. And how close does what they make get to a body, and whose — their own, someone they love, a stranger's, or none. Under both run the two walks: the cloth ladder, seven rungs from a two-dollar swatch to a new fabric, and the mats programme, four steps from a sample to an annual commitment. Customers walk up, walk down, leave, and come back. Nothing here was invented; it was counted, then named.

Where they stand

Two walks. One for cloth, one for mats.

Every customer stands on one of these. The cloth ladder is walked by makers of every size, from a swatch to a fabric of their own. The mats programme is walked by industrial buyers, and it has no swatch on it.

The cloth ladder seven rungs · every number per fabric

    Two standing rules travel with it. Custom print and custom colour are per fabric; no site-wide minimum ever ships. And custom print sits below bulk — it is the cheapest rung on which a fabric becomes the maker's own, and the one that recruits, not the one that rewards.

    The mats programme four steps · no swatch

      Twenty-two sorbent customers, every one industrial, every one buying to a spec. They arrive through each other — Brady brought the constellation — and they buy on their own rhythm, not ours. In 2026 they are the core of the business.

        How they arrive

        Two intakes, not one funnel. Makers come through Wazoodle, most of them convinced by other customers before they write a word — "after doing a lot of research… I decided on this ProCool Jersey because of the reviews I read." Manufacturers and distributors arrive at the top, with a part number, and never see the ladder. And the honest position on how the largest accounts arrived: of the thirty-two biggest, one shows a visible swatch-first entry — because a swatch generates no email. Mostly, we don't know. The record only starts when someone writes in.

        Before the nine

        First question: what do they do with the cloth?Ask · make · carry · cut · specify. Every customer sits in exactly one.

        We asked it of all 1,297 businesses in our customer register. 1,172 make something from it. 85 buy it to a spec into a product line. 21 cut it into someone else's product. 13 carry it onward as fabric. 8 exist only as a request. Four refused to choose between asking and specifying — they arrived with a spec that didn't exist yet — and that is the point: asking isn't a fifth kind of customer. It's the first thing every kind does.

        Second question: how close does it get, to whose body?Their own · someone they love · a stranger · a thing. The reason they came is folded into the answer.

        Where the money is, things and loved bodies lead. Where the people are — our book of customer stories — the own body leads by a mile: 306 named makers against 170, 179 and 79. Our money comes from things; our people come from skin. And why they came sorts itself onto the same four: it hurt me, it must not hurt her, someone out there has nothing, I'm done throwing things away.

        The ladder is walked both ways.1,047 stand at wholesale. 181 reached a roll. 58 reached bulk. 6 reached development.

        Forty businesses have bought at both doors. Fifteen went up — Wazoodle first, then AKAS Tex. Twenty-five went down — AKAS Tex first, then Wazoodle for samples, colours, the next idea. The climb we used to think of as the story isn't the common direction. Nobody is pushed. The next rung is visible, and the family stood on every one of them first.

        The top feeds the bottom.Every fabric on the shelf began as somebody's request.

        Someone asks; the mill makes it; the leftover goes on the shelf; a maker buys a yard of it next week. Fifty-two of our hundred and two fabrics carry the name of the person who asked for them. So the ladder isn't a ladder. It's a loop — and the loop keeps running whether or not the person who asked stays.

        The distance of love.Something made for a child is made safer than for yourself.

        The people who make for their own body write six words and stop. The people who make for a body they love write pages, rank fabrics, name what failed, try again two weeks later. The closer the body, the shorter the review; the more loved the body, the longer the care. A maker's own skin and her child's stand at the same point, and everything commercial lies between.

        Where we draw the line.We don't ship to India, China, Pakistan or Sri Lanka, and we don't assist orders routed there.

        There is a side of this industry that comes for the development, takes the sample runs and the iterations, and has the result made elsewhere for less. Sometimes the biggest name in the room; sometimes a maker who was ours until the investor cheque arrived. We know it exists. We've fed it more than once. Customers may send our cloth wherever they like — they do it without us.

        Where the names come from.Our customer register knows who bought. Our book of customer stories knows what for.

        The register holds every business Wazoodle or AKAS Tex knows by name — 1,297, each placed as one of the nine on 8 September 2026, with the evidence written on the record. The book of stories holds the people the register can't see — the guest checkouts, the reviews — 727 of them named across twenty-six communities. Thirty-two people are in both. Real names, on an internal page.

        The closer the body, the shorter the chain.Nobody carries, cuts or specifies for their own body.

        Cross the two questions and twenty combinations are possible. Seven hold real numbers of people. Distributors and manufacturers pile up almost entirely on things; makers spread across all four bodies. As the body gets nearer, it becomes certain that the person who makes the thing is the person buying the cloth — and that the person who knits the cloth is the one who answers her. Every name on this page is a name because of that.

        Coming home

        You've met the nine. Here's what you already knew.

        Everyone begins as an Explorer.

        Every email, every call, every knock is the same move: someone asking. That is why we have always given the best of ourselves to whoever writes in — we don't know yet which of the nine they are, and neither do they. The archetype is what the question becomes once it has a body and a rung.

        The nine are distances, not types.

        Nobody is born a Guardian. The same human can be three of these across ten years — a Guardian who becomes an Innovator who becomes a loose thread — and the warp she left is still on the shelf, feeding the next Guardian. The leftover outlives the leaving.

        Nobody came because they couldn't find a fabric.

        They came because what they found hurt, failed, cost too much, or wasn't safe enough for the person it was for. Keep me safe. Help the ones who can't. Stop wasting me. Let me earn. I hurt. This isn't working. Every fabric on the shelf is a reply to one of those — and I couldn't find you barely appears, because someone answered the phone.

        Why now.

        Every account that ever moved — up, down, back — moved for one of two reasons: a new product, or supply trouble. That is the opening line of every conversation worth having. And the fingerprint that says this one matters: a fresh AK style number in the record. A fabric made for them.
